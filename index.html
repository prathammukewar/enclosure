      How to play

      Enclosure is a game for two players on a 19 by 19 grid of points. You grow a drawing of edges, fence in area, and cut your opponent's edges. After 120 edges, the player with more points wins.

      The start

      Blue and Red each begin with one edge on the middle row. Blue's runs from the left side, a10, to d10. Red's runs from the right side, s10, to p10.

      The ends of your edges are your nodes. Every new edge starts at one of them.

      Turns

      Blue goes first and places one edge. After that, players take turns placing two edges each: Red two, Blue two, and so on. The game lasts exactly 120 edges, so Blue's final turn is a single edge. Each player places 60.

      Placing an edge

      Pick one of your nodes and draw an edge to any point at most 3 steps away across and at most 3 steps up or down. That's the 7 by 7 square around the node. Any angle is fine. The far end becomes a new node, or joins a node of yours already there.

      Two limits apply to your own drawing:

      • You can't end an edge on the middle of one of your own edges.
      • An edge can't pass through one of your own nodes.

      Apart from that, your edges may cross each other.

      Breaking edges

      If your new edge touches one of your opponent's edges, their edge breaks and disappears. Crossing it counts. So does just touching it: ending your edge on it, or on one of its nodes. Any of their nodes left with no edges disappears too.

      Each edge can break only one enemy edge. If it would touch two or more, you can't place it. Running through a node where two enemy edges meet touches both, so that isn't allowed either.

      New edges are protected for one turn. You can't break an edge your opponent placed on their previous turn. On this site new edges have a light center line, so you can tell which ones are safe for now.

      Area and score

      Your area is everything your edges fence in, measured exactly in grid squares. A triangle across half a square counts 0.5.

      At the end of every turn, both players add their current area to their score. If you hold 18 and your opponent holds 10, then when a turn ends you gain 18 and they gain 10. Area keeps paying every turn you hold it, and stops paying the moment a break opens it up.

      Scores update after edges 1, 3, 5, and so on up to 119, and once more after edge 120. That's 61 updates. When the game ends, the higher score wins. Equal scores are a draw.

      Questions

      What if some of my area sits inside other area of mine?
      It counts once. A triangle inside your quadrilateral adds nothing beyond the quadrilateral.
      Do loops made by my own edges crossing count?
      Yes. Any region your edges fence in counts, wherever the corners are.
      What if my area surrounds my opponent's?
      You get your area as if they weren't there, and they get theirs as if you weren't there.
      Can my drawing split into separate pieces?
      Yes. If your opponent breaks the edge holding two parts together, both parts stay, and you can keep building from either one.
      Does the edge of the board fence anything in?
      No. Only your own edges enclose area.
      Can a player lose every edge?
      Not in normal play. The edges you placed on your last turn are protected while your opponent moves.
      What if I have no legal edge?
      It almost never happens. On this site your edge is skipped, and it still counts toward the 120.

      Clocks

      Games can be played with a clock. The tournament setting is 1 minute plus 15 seconds: your clock runs during your turn, and each time you finish a turn you gain 15 seconds. If your clock runs out, the rest of your turn is skipped, the edges you placed earlier in that turn lose their protection, and you get 15 seconds for your next turn. Running out of time doesn't lose the game on its own.

      Notation on this site

      Columns are a to s from left to right and rows are 1 to 19 from bottom to top. An edge is written as its two ends, like d10-f12. An x in the middle, like f12xh13, means it broke an enemy edge.

      House rules on this site

      Everything above is the standard game. When you start a game here you can also change a few things, for practice or for fun. Games with changed rules say so on the play screen, and their links carry the rules with them.

      • A 13 by 13 board, and fewer edges each, for quicker games.
      • A reach of 2 or 4 instead of 3.
      • Three or four players, each starting on a different side, or two teams of two. Teammates can't touch each other's edges, and a team's score is its players' scores added up.
      • No protection for new edges.
      • Border walls: the edge of the board counts as one of your walls. The largest open part of the board is the outside, and everything else your edges and the border close off counts.
      • Running out of time can skip just one edge instead of the rest of the turn.
      • Handicap edges: extra edges in a player's first turn.

        How this site works

        The whole thing runs in your browser: plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no server of its own. Here is how the trickier parts work.

        Measuring area exactly

        Every edge joins two grid points, so all the contact tests use whole numbers and are exact. Area is harder, because a player's own edges can cross. The site splits every edge at its crossings, giving a flat map of corners and pieces. Walking around each face of that map with the shoelace formula gives its area. Faces that wind one way are fenced in; the one outer face winds the other way. If one of your pieces sits inside another of your pens, it's only counted once.

        The tests check this against a second method that shares no code with it: cut the board into thin vertical slabs at every corner and crossing, fill in from the outside, and add up the trapezoids that the fill never reaches.

        Checking the rules

        The rules engine is tested against a second, deliberately simple version written straight from the rule text. Thousands of random games, including every house-rule variant, compare the two on every candidate edge.

        The computer player

        It plans a whole turn at once. It ranks first edges by what they do right away and what they set up, tries the best second edges for each, and scores the result. The score adds the points gap, the area each side holds, what the opponent could break on their very next turn, and how exposed each cell is: a wall close to enemy nodes is more likely to go, and a cell is lost if any of its outside walls goes.

        The levels were tuned with computer-against-computer matches. Hard beats Medium about four games in five, and Medium beats Easy about nine in ten. Expert searches wider again with weights tuned by an automatic search over those matches.

        Puzzles you can trust

        Each puzzle comes from a computer game. The best turn is found by trying every first edge and every second edge that could matter, then replaying the best candidates exactly until nothing left could beat them. A separate check then plays out every legal pair of edges on the engine alone and confirms the answer. Block puzzles are checked the same way: every edge you could place, against every enemy edge that could cut you after it.

        Plan-ahead puzzles are the exception. Four edges in a row have far too many combinations to try them all, so the target is the best plan a search found, and it's always more than two separate best turns would get. The puzzle asks you to reach it, not to prove nothing beats it.

        Online play

        Online games connect browsers directly with WebRTC. A public PeerJS server only introduces them. The host's browser keeps the official move list and passes moves on to the other players and anyone watching.
